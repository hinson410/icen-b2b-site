Buying Guide

3-Part vs 5-Part Differential: Which Hematology Analyzer Do You Need?

A 3-part analyzer sorts white cells into lymphocytes, mid-cells and granulocytes. A 5-part analyzer separates neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils, and reports each population as a percentage and an absolute count. The deciding question is not which instrument is better, but whether your clinicians need eosinophil and basophil information from the same tube. ICEN supplies four 3-part Mindray models (BC-10, BC-20, BC-20s, BC-30s) and eight 5-part models (BC-5000, BC-5120, BC-5150, BC-5380, BC-5390CRP, BC-6800, BC-760 and the BC-7600 Series).

The reports

What each differential actually contains

The difference is not the number of parameters printed on a result sheet. It is which cell populations remain merged inside a single figure, because a merged figure cannot be trended.

Read the fourth row carefully. A 3-part report is not a subset that can be upgraded later by software. The middle group is a measurement limitation of the instrument, so a MID value that rises tells you something in that group changed, but not which of the three populations moved.

Limits

What a 3-part report cannot answer

These are the questions that force a laboratory up to a 5-part instrument. Each one is a reporting gap, not a workflow preference.

  • Eosinophil trend. Allergy follow-up, asthma monitoring and parasite burden work all read the eosinophil count across visits. In a 3-part report the eosinophil is inside MID, mixed with monocytes and basophils, so the trend cannot be isolated.
  • Basophils at all. A 3-part differential does not report basophils as a separate value at any point.
  • Separating monocytosis from eosinophilia. A raised MID value is compatible with either. The 5-part report answers it directly.
  • A true absolute neutrophil count. The granulocyte group in a 3-part report includes neutrophils together with the eosinophils and basophils that were swept into it, so a count derived from the granulocyte percentage is an absolute granulocyte count rather than a pure neutrophil count. Oncology protocols and febrile neutropenia pathways are written against the neutrophil figure.

If any of those four statements describes work your laboratory already does, the differential decides the purchase before throughput or price are discussed.

Fit

When a 3-part analyzer is the right purchase

A 3-part instrument is a correct choice when the case mix is screening rather than diagnostic, and when no clinician on site asks for eosinophil or basophil reporting.

The common profile is an outpatient clinic or a small private laboratory running routine CBCs before consultations, pre-operative assessment or general health checks, where results feed a decision to treat, refer or repeat rather than to manage a haematology patient. In that setting a 5-part report is generated but not acted on differently.

Two details make the 3-part tier less of a compromise than it sounds. The BC-20s and BC-20 both run from a 9 microlitre whole-blood sample, which is low enough for capillary work with children and for difficult draws. The BC-30s reports 21 parameters at 70 samples per hour and includes WiFi and bi-directional LIS, so the workflow around the instrument can be as complete as a larger laboratory's even though the differential stays at three parts.

The honest counter-argument is expansion. If a new oncology contract, an allergy clinic or a paediatric ward is plausible within the life of the instrument, buying 5-part once costs less than replacing a 3-part analyzer in year two. That judgement belongs to whoever owns the service plan, not to the analyzer specification.

Fit

When you need the fifth population

Move to 5-part when any of the following is true of your workload: the laboratory supports inpatient wards where differentials drive treatment decisions; an oncology day unit monitors patients between cycles; a paediatric ward works up neonatal sepsis or jaundice; an allergy or parasitology service reads eosinophil counts; or the laboratory accepts referred samples from clinics that expect a full differential.

Beyond the differential, two capabilities appear only at the upper end of the 5-part range on this list. The BC-6800 reports 125 tests per hour from a 100-tube autoloader, counts platelets by fluorescence flow cytometry (PLT-O) alongside impedance, supports body fluid analysis and stores up to 100,000 results with bi-directional LIS. The BC-7600 Series adds an integrated ESR module so that CBC and ESR come from the same tube in a single test, which removes a separate sedimentation rack from the workflow.

The number that decides paediatric work is sample volume, not the differential

Specification

What to put in the RFQ

A quotation can only be accurate against a stated workload. Six points remove most of the rework:

  • Average and peak-hour CBC volume, plus whether morning rounds concentrate the load.
  • Whether eosinophil and basophil reporting is required, and by which department.
  • Minimum acceptable sample volume, and whether capillary samples must be accepted.
  • LIS interface type and how many results the instrument must store.
  • Number of routine reagents and their shelf life, since this is the recurring cost rather than the purchase price.
  • Destination country, because product availability and market authorisation depend on the market.

FAQ

Questions buyers ask about the differential

Is a 3-part differential enough for a small clinic?

Usually yes, if the result only needs to support a decision to treat, refer or repeat. It stops being enough as soon as a clinician asks for an eosinophil or basophil count, or trends a differential across visits.

Can a 3-part analyzer report an absolute neutrophil count?

It reports a granulocyte percentage, and the granulocyte group includes neutrophils together with the eosinophils and basophils that were merged into it. A count derived from that percentage is an absolute granulocyte count, not a pure neutrophil count.

Do I need 5-part for body fluid analysis?

Within the ICEN range, body fluid analysis is published for the BC-6800. The lower 5-part models should not be assumed to include it.

Which models run on 9 microlitres of blood?

The BC-20 and the BC-20s, both 3-part instruments. The smallest published volume among the 5-part models on this list is 15 microlitres, on the BC-5000 and BC-5150.

Can the differential be upgraded later by software?

No. The three-part grouping is a measurement limitation of the instrument, not a reporting setting, so the fifth population requires a different analyzer.

How do I get a quotation?

Send the average and peak-hour CBC volume, whether eosinophil and basophil reporting is needed, the minimum acceptable sample volume, the LIS interface and the destination country through the Request a Quote form.
